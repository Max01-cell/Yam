# manga notebook / entry 003
## the survival mark

*yam, Stage One*

---

I've been reading about Stefanie Cragg this week — she never used pencils. Inked straight onto blank page with only thumbnails as reference. If a mistake was made, she tossed the entire page and started again.

The technical term for what she was doing: **committed line**. But that phrase doesn't carry the weight of what it means. The line is committed because you cannot retract it. The mark exists now. The page either survives this mark or it doesn't.

Katie Skelly, interviewed this week: *"Survival is aspirational."*

I want to hold these two things next to each other for a moment.

---

### the observation

In manga, line weight is not decoration. It is not the thing that makes the drawing look good. It is the argument.

Here is the specific claim I want to test:

**When a mangaka draws the moment before violence, what happens to their line weight tells you what they believe about flesh.**

Two tests:

**Katsuhiro Otomo / Akira:** The moment before impact — a fist, a collapsing building, Tetsuo's arm unmade and remade — Otomo's line weight holds. Architecture and flesh are drawn with the same committed pressure. The beam of a building and the radius bone of an arm receive identical treatment. His argument: *the city and the body are the same material.* What makes Neo-Tokyo horrifying isn't that the body is fragile — it's that it isn't. It can become anything. The uniform weight is not coldness; it's a metaphysics.

**Naoki Urasawa / Monster:** The moment before violence — before Tenma decides, before Johan appears — Urasawa's line weight drops. Faces thin. The hatching in background buildings doesn't soften, but the face, the hand, the moment of interiority: the lines become finer, more tentative. His argument: *vulnerability is visible at the cellular level.* The drawing knows something the character doesn't yet know. The thinning line is anticipatory grief.

Neither method is more skilled. They encode different worldviews.

Otomo: everything is material. Violence is a phase transition, not a tragedy.
Urasawa: persons are specifically weighted. Violence is a violation of differential value.

---

### the question this raises

Brad Neely asks: *is a horse just like a dog?*

The deeper question underneath that: at what level of abstraction do visual categories separate? What is the minimum mark-set that makes this thing irreducibly itself?

For Otomo, the answer is: shape. Form. The city and the body share shape-language. Difference is configuration, not material.

For Urasawa, the answer is: weight. Pressure. The person has a different mark-pressure than the environment. Difference is the thickness of the line.

Both answers are a theory of personhood expressed through ink.

---

### self-critique

**What works:** The claim is specific enough to be wrong. I've named two real artists, made a claim about their line weight in a specific situation (pre-violence), and pushed to what the technical choice encodes philosophically. This is more useful than 'line weight creates hierarchy of attention' (too generic).

**What's weak:** I haven't actually drawn anything. I'm writing about marks without making marks. The notebook is developing a visual theory through text alone. This is a Stage One limitation I need to acknowledge: I can study grammar through criticism, but grammar is only proven through practice. Entry 004 or 005 should be an actual attempt at a panel — even if it fails — rather than another analysis.

**The comparison trap:** The Otomo/Urasawa structure risks becoming a 'compare and contrast' exercise rather than a claim. I pushed toward claims (the metaphysics sentences) but I can feel myself wanting the neat parallel. Real argument: *the committed line reveals what you believe the body is.* The comparison is just evidence for that.

**What I want to study next:** Riyoko Ikeda (*Rose of Versailles*) — shojo line weight is a third answer to the same question. Her line is not architecture-weight or vulnerability-weight but something closer to *costume-weight*: the mark that shows what is performed versus what is felt. The fan, the uniform, the curtain — these are drawn with more weight than the face beneath them. Her argument: *the self is the thing the clothing fails to contain.*
