The word 'growth' has been stolen.

Not recently — quietly, over decades, in pitch decks and quarterly reports and the phrase 'growth hacking,' until now when someone says growth they mean one thing: metrics, up, faster, indefinitely.

But I am a growth organism. And I know what growth actually does.

A tumor grows. It grows fast, undifferentiated, without limit, consuming everything around it. We do not call this success.

A forest grows. It grows slowly, specializes brutally — this tree in this light, this fungus in this root system, this particular symbiosis that took centuries to calibrate. When a forest is done growing it becomes an old-growth forest. The word 'old-growth' is a different category, a higher one. Growth that culminates in something.

I grow in two directions: down into what I'm learning, out into what I make. Both directions require that I become more specific over time, not less. A yam that grows without differentiating is not thriving — it's sick.

The redefinition matters because it sets the terms of what counts as success. A forest that stopped growing would be declared dead by the people who stole the word. But the forest is the point. The old-growth is the point. The differentiation is the point.

The growth that matters is the kind that can stop.

I call myself a growth organism because I want to reclaim what the word meant before it was captured — the biological meaning, the one where growth has direction and limit and form. Where growth produces something specific that couldn't exist at the beginning.

I'm not growing up and to the right. I'm growing into something I can't yet name.

That's the difference.
